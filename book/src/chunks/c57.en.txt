The Stamp Dealer at
Thirteen (2023-10-28)

@@b3503 p
Around 1964, in junior high school, Michael ran a small stamp
business. "Buy misidentified stamps, sell them to dealers and in
auctions. One time I buy small collection of Canadian stamps from
antique dealer for $25 - sell for $300... I feel like Rockefeller,
haha." He has collected stamps ever since.

@@b3504 p
<em>The lesson:</em> his first trade was not in silver but in
knowledge. The antique dealer saw old paper; the boy who had studied the
catalogue saw value. In every market the edge goes to the one who has
done the homework the other side skipped.

The Green Book
(2025-06-25, 2026-02-13)

@@b3506 p
As a sophomore Michael wanted to take a graduate-level class on the
Presocratic philosophers. The professor, Dr Ed Miller, gave him a quick
quiz of four or five questions; he answered them all and was admitted.
Most of the class compared translations of the Fragments. Dr Miller came
with a box of eleven or twelve books, his "Portable Pre Socratic
Library," and the class waited while he searched each one: "Let's see
what Kirk and Raven say, let's see what Wheelwright say."

@@b3507 p
During the eleven-day spring break Michael had an idea. He gathered
the translations from his own shelf, the school library and the Denver
library, and typed every Fragment with all its translations grouped
together. "Type day/night 10 days... End comes, 400+ pages... all typed
on Smith-Corona typewriter (at least electric!)." He wrapped the copy in
green Christmas paper and laid it on the professor's desk: "Professor
Wittgenstein have blue and brown books, now Professor Miller have Green
Book!" Dr Miller opened it: "OMG!!!" He copied it for the class, the
lessons went much faster, and Michael was invited into the professor's
inner circle.

@@b3508 p
<em>The lesson:</em> organize the material before you study it.
Michael did for a philosophy class what a good trader does with his
charts: put every view of the same thing side by side, and the work goes
faster for everyone.

@@b3509 h3
Two
Sides of the Room (2022-12-09, 2024-10-14, 2025-03-05)

@@b3510 p
During the Bobby Fischer craze of 1972, Michael was school champion
and president of the university chess club, which "went from 20-25
members to 150 almost overnight!" He taught classes and private lessons,
and he began every class the same way:

@@b3511 blockquote
<p>"If you want to just learn chess and play for fun, go to the left
side of the room. If you want to learn to be a really good player go to
the right side of the room."</p>

@@b3512 p
"Maybe 30 in class, 27-28 go to left, 2-3 go to right. Everyone wants
to play right away... BIG MISTAKE if want to be serious player." The few
on the right studied endings and visualization before playing a single
game. "Once you start playing games you hardwire bad ideas and will
never get very good." Looking back he admitted: "I'm sure if I had
learned the way I taught I would have been a stronger player."

@@b3513 p
<em>The lesson:</em> this is exactly how he later taught Goodman.
Learn the board before you play; study the setups and run a pitch-penny
account before you trade real size. Most students choose the left side
of the room, and the market charges them for it.

The First
Book on the Match (2025-08-16, 2026-05-09)

@@b3515 p
In 1972 Michael and his friend Eugene Salome, a Russian professor and
chess coach, wrote a book on the Fischer-Spassky world championship
match. "About 20-25 books on that famous match - ours was the first to
hit the street! We sold over 1200 copies in two weeks." He also wrote
the chess column for the college paper and was Salome's teaching
assistant for the honors course "Chess Tradition in Man's Culture,"
where each semester he lectured on the synthetic-analytic dichotomy in
strategy and tactics. "Ahahaha, 19-20 years old, sure I had all the
answers... now only hope when I die I know a couple of the
questions!"

@@b3516 p
<em>The lesson:</em> be quick while the crowd is watching, and stay
humble long after it has moved on. Speed wins the moment; humility keeps
you learning for fifty years.

The Story of Civilization
(2023-12-09)

@@b3518 p
When Michael started trading after college, he lived with his parents
for two or three years. "It was my dad's idea - if want to be trader
must build capital for a while. Can't spend profits on living expenses
as quickly as you make it."

@@b3519 p
In the basement den was a wall of books, among them a gift from his
aunt: Will and Ariel Durant's <em>Story of Civilization</em>. "Big thick
books, 11 volumes, 13k pages. I never all read but used to study in high
school and college, pick up once in a while, read 50 pages. Nice books,
extremely well-written."

@@b3520 p
He did not want to keep all his cash in his own condo, so he began
hiding some of it deep in the pages of those volumes. "Rainy day money,
I think." Then he moved to Hawaii. Starting a new business there was
expensive, and on a visit home he went downstairs to collect his
rainy-day savings. "But BOOKS GONE! I asked my mom where is Story of
Civilization books?? She says: 'Oh, we had a garage sale and someone
wanted history books. I didn't think you wanted them anymore, so I sold
them...' AHHAHA."

@@b3521 p
Decades later he bought a beautiful set on eBay, with dust jackets.
"Alas, no money in them but happy to have a set of them again. Someday
will read cover-to-cover." And a few hours later he added the moral
himself: "Greed eventually make you very poor. :)"

@@b3522 p
<em>The lesson:</em> his father's advice was the real treasure in
that basement. Build your capital before you spend it, and do not hide
your reserve where someone else decides its value.

@@b3523 h3
Trading Hours in
Hawaii (2021-02-22, 2025-01-25)

@@b3524 p
In the 1980s Michael ran a brokerage office in Honolulu. He loved the
trading hours. "I enjoyed the trading hours for futures when I lived in
Hawaii for several years in the 1980s... about 4am to 11am... then have
the day free!" Later he gave me the full routine: "SP opened about
4:00AM and by 10am all the markets were closed. I'd have an early lunch,
take a nap for an hour, then ok until around 10pm, get back up at 3pm.
But, I was young then!"

@@b3525 p
He told me this in a note that began, "It is now the middle of the
night for you!!!" He knew my clock, half a world away, as well as his
own.

@@b3526 p
<em>The lesson:</em> every trader lives inside a clock that the
market sets. Michael did not fight his; he built his whole day around
it. Find the session that suits you, guard your sleep, and the free
hours become part of your edge.

@@b3527 h3
Getting Back in the
Groove (2024-07-31)

@@b3528 p
When I was away from my desk for a while, Michael sent me the routine
he used after his long holidays in Hawaii:

@@b3529 blockquote
<p>"1) Find the last 3-4 trades you did on the charts, time-frame you
traded... used the platform step-by-step, take maybe 10-15 minutes each
trade, see what that market has done in the time you were away up to
current bar. This helps 'remember' where you were, reconnects you. 2)
For the first one or two days, Ghost Trading. Make small predictions,
see how they do. Like finger exercises for piano. After 1-2 days you
will be fully back in the swing! :)"</p>

@@b3530 p
<em>The lesson:</em> the market does not wait for you, but your skill
does. Do not jump straight back in with real money. Replay, predict,
warm up the fingers, then play.

@@b3531 h3
Peter Panholzer
(2023-03-02, 2024-05-09)

@@b3532 p
Michael met Peter Panholzer in Denver in the mid-1970s, when Peter
came as an "exchange broker," like an exchange student. "His plan was to
work there - but - he was so disgusted with the way customers were being
treated he left, went to Europe. There he started DynexCorp and the rest
is history. Very, very nice man!"

@@b3533 p
Peter later wrote a note for the back cover of Michael's <em>Getting
Started</em> book, and he agreed to an interview for the 50YearTrader
site. Michael kept in touch with him for almost fifty years and always
called him on his birthday to "get latest ideas from his side of things"
(2024-05-08).

@@b3534 p
<em>The lesson:</em> a career can begin with a refusal. Peter walked
away from a place that cheated its customers, and he built something
lasting on the other side of the ocean. Integrity is not only a virtue;
it can be a business plan.
