23. The boy who missed the
contest

@@b2915 p
<em>Charlie looked where nobody else looked.</em>

@@b2916 p
The Bache home office once sent every branch a promotional film. The
brokers and staff gathered to watch it on an old 16mm projector. It was
narrated by the Bache CEO, who began with his own story. He had been in
a state math contest for a scholarship. He could not afford school and
wanted it badly, so he studied extra hard. Only one other student could
beat him. On the morning of the contest, that other student was in a
traffic accident on the way. He was fine, but in hospital, and could not
come to the contest. The CEO won the scholarship. At the end of the film
he remembered this and said, "I was lucky that day. Who knows where I
would be if not for the scholarship? Good luck for me, bad luck for
somebody else."

@@b2917 p
The projector stopped and the lights came on. Charlie said: "I'd much
rather know what happened to the boy who missed the contest!"

@@b2918 p
Michael says this story is similar to the raccoon story and to the
day a car hit Charlie's Corvair while they studied charts. Charlie
thought: what would have happened if? And: "I am more interested in how
the boy who missed his chance handled life." "No one else looked from
that perspective, ever! Just Charlie."

@@b2919 p
<strong>What it means.</strong> The winner's story is told by
everyone. The more useful lesson is usually in the story of the one who
lost.

@@b2920 p
<strong>Try this.</strong> Write a short post-mortem on your worst
trade as if you were the person who lost, not the one who learned.

24. The rocks on the table

@@b2922 p
<em>A dream, September 2025.</em>

@@b2923 p
Michael rarely dreams of Charlie, though Charlie sometimes talks to
him. One night he did. They were in the Bache lunchroom, at a small
table for two. On the table were five or six rocks, crystals and mineral
chunks, ranging from the size of a football to the size of a silver
dollar. Charlie arranged them in a row from largest to smallest.

@@b2924 p
"This is the way most people arrange them, but that's wrong," he
said.

@@b2925 p
He took the smallest one and put it in the middle, and put the others
around it.

@@b2926 p
"This is the correct way: the largest ones protect the smallest, and
the smallest keeps the others in proper order!"

@@b2928 p
Figure: The rocks in a row from largest to smallest, and Charlie's
way, with the smallest in the middle and the others around it.

@@b2929 p
<strong>What it means.</strong> A risk, a small stop, a tiny rule can
hold a larger structure in order, if the large things are arranged to
protect it.

@@b2930 p
<strong>Try this.</strong> Draw your own trading plan as a circle.
Put your smallest rule in the center, and name the three things around
it that protect it.

25. Why we invented sports

@@b2932 p
<em>The World Series, 1977.</em>

@@b2933 p
In the fall of 1977, the World Series was being played by the New
York Yankees and the Los Angeles Dodgers. Bache had a small TV in the
corner of the lunchroom so people could watch. Michael told Charlie
that, when you thought about it, baseball, football, hockey and
basketball were really silly.

@@b2934 p
"No, Dad, they serve a real purpose. We create games to play to
highlight and develop very specific skill sets. Gambling is sort of the
same: we create risks in order to take them. It is part of our
evolution, to develop our brains and bodies.

@@b2935 p
"You know how the immune system works? If we get sick it kicks in to
cure us. Each time it is called it gets stronger and stronger. We're
doing the same thing for our brains and bodies with sports and gambling.
Making them stronger and taking us a little further down the
evolutionary path.

@@b2936 p
"Sometimes things aren't what they seem at first, Dad. You have to
see them in a wider perspective. If you only look at the wall of a
building close up, you'll never see the city around it."

@@b2937 p
<strong>What it means.</strong> A game is a small, safe version of a
risk you will meet later. Trading is a game with real stakes, so train
for it like one.

@@b2938 p
<strong>Try this.</strong> Treat your next ten trades as practice
reps with a score. Judge them by process points, not by profit.

26. Everything is relative

@@b2940 p
<em>Paperboy versus the field.</em>

@@b2941 p
One day Charlie and Michael talked about work and how hard it is to
really make money. Michael told Charlie that at twelve he had a paper
route: eighty-five papers on weekdays and one hundred and ten on the
Sunday edition, delivered in the middle of the night, riding uphill in
winter snow. He worked two hours a day, three on Sunday, plus ten hours
a month collecting from subscribers, and made about $30 a month. He
expected sympathy.

@@b2942 p
"Dad, during the season of planting, growing and harvesting, I worked
twelve to sixteen hours a day in the field. Hot sun, hard labor like in
prison. I made no money, I got only food to eat, clothes to wear and
shelter. I trade with you, a soft job and easy money!"

@@b2943 p
Michael adds a line from Xenophanes: sugar is sweet only by
convention.

@@b2944 p
<strong>What it means.</strong> Charlie never forgot where he
started. He thought of trading as a soft job and treated its profits
with gratitude.

@@b2945 p
<strong>Try this.</strong> Write down the hardest job you ever had.
Read it before your next large trade.

27. Last Chance, Colorado

@@b2947 p
<em>No moral. Just a good memory.</em>

@@b2948 p
Charlie and Michael were returning from the dog track in Byers,
Colorado, about forty miles east of Denver, out on the prairie. It was
pitch black, with no markers or signs to say where they were. After a
few miles Charlie said, "Dad, are you sure we're headed in the right
direction back to Denver?"

@@b2949 p
"I'm not sure, Charlie. Nothing looks familiar of what little I can
see." They kept driving. After another ten miles or so they saw a sign:
WELCOME TO LAST CHANCE, COLORADO!

@@b2950 p
They both laughed a little. Charlie said, "Dad, I think this is your
last chance to turn around before we wind up in Kansas with Dorothy and
Toto!"

@@b2951 p
Michael wrote: "Good memories... fifty years ago close now."

@@b2952 p
<strong>Try this.</strong> No exercise. Tell someone a funny story
today.

28. Everything is a 1-2-3

@@b2954 p
<em>Thrust, parry, thrust, canine style.</em>

@@b2955 p
In September 2026 Michael sent a small story about two little dogs
out on a walk together, an older one and a younger one.

@@b2956 p
The older dog stopped at a tree and marked it. The younger one ran
over and marked the same spot. The older one went straight back and
marked it again. Michael laughed and said, "1-2-3." The reply came back:
"I guess Mr. Goodman is correct: everything is 1-2-3!"

@@b2957 p
Michael called it thrust, parry, thrust, canine style.

@@b2959 p
Figure: Thrust, parry, thrust: the two dogs marking the tree as a
1-2-3.

@@b2960 p
<strong>What it means.</strong> The pattern Charlie described is not
only on charts. It is in any action, response and counter-response.

@@b2961 p
<strong>Try this.</strong> Today, spot one 1-2-3 that has nothing to
do with markets.

Where I Trade From

@@b2963 p
Charlie's stories in this part of the book are about the inner game.
These are my notes on the same game, written to you over five years,
Michael. I did not plan them as a system. Reading them together, I see
that they became one.

@@b2964 h3
The mind is programmed
backwards

@@b2965 p
In April 2021 I wrote that we are supposed to buy at the greatest
fear and sell at the greatest happiness, and that we so often do the
opposite. "Its a bad strategy our unconscious mind will automatically
follow . Our mind program its give us a very bad out put . But we can
reprogram it slowly ." (April 24, 2021)

@@b2966 p
The word I care about now is "slowly." There is no switch. There is
only practice.

@@b2967 p
In August 2021 I described what fear does to a person: "fear when
it's come. the real power inside man disappears. evaporation." (August
8, 2021) The skill is still inside you. Fear simply makes it
unavailable.

@@b2968 h3
The cycle of one trade

@@b2969 p
In May 2024 I finally wrote down the cycle I had felt for years.

@@b2970 p
Before the trade, we are excited and sure it is a winner. During the
trade, if it does not move, we think we made a mistake and feel sad; if
it moves, we forget to raise the stop, or we fear a bad stop, and we
"Run fast or not run at all." After the trade, a win makes us rush to
find the next one for more money. A loss raises the emotional
temperature: nothing will work, and an excellent trade passes by.

@@b2971 p
"Thats emotions cycles like blood circulation." (May 25, 2024)

@@b2973 p
Figure: The emotional cycle of one trade: before, during, after.
