The rule

@@b2189 p
When everyone is selling, it means buy. When everyone is buying, it
means sell. The crowd is never wrong about the move that has already
happened. It is wrong about the next one.

@@b2190 h2
The 6&amp;6 Under the
Microscope (2021)

@@b2191 p
There are several types of 6&amp;6 Setup, and each one affects the
profit we can make. In reality some of them will produce a loss, and it
is hard to know which type gives the maximum profit. In this paper I try
to find the way to the most profitable type.

@@b2192 h3
Method and Logical Model

@@b2193 p
A target this large needs a strong diagnostic tool. I use Game
Theory, and especially the Nash equilibrium, to examine the 6&amp;6
types.

@@b2194 p
My logical model is simple. Some cases produce a successful 6&amp;6
and others produce a failed one. Our aim is to find the cause that gives
a success instead of a failure. So I study the cases and their causes,
and ask why each one succeeds or fails.

@@b2195 h3
Mass Behavior and
Constant Conjunction

@@b2196 p
The 6&amp;6 pattern is just a reflection of mass behavior. Every case
of the 6&amp;6 reflects a different type of mass behavior. We do not
need to know the "why" behind that behavior. As Hume put it:

@@b2197 blockquote
<p>"Reason alone cannot be a motive to the will, but rather is the
'slave of the passions'."</p>
<p>"We never actually perceive that one event causes another but only
experience the 'constant conjunction' of events."</p>

@@b2198 p
By experiencing the constant conjunction of events behind mass
behavior, we can induce a logical response. That logical response can be
framed as a Nash equilibrium.

@@b2199 h3
Why the Nash Equilibrium
Matters

@@b2200 blockquote
<p>"However, one doesn't need to be a wayward genius to understand the
idea of a Nash equilibrium. We have seen that the payoffs in a game are
chosen to make it tautological that rational players will seek to
maximize their average payoff. This would be easy if players knew what
strategies their opponents were going to choose." — Ken Binmore</p>

@@b2201 p
In a Nash equilibrium, rational players seek to maximize their
profit. A professional trader who makes a living from trading wants
exactly that. He looks for a great target and a great movement.

@@b2202 p
The evolutionary reading of this is very clear at the Intersection.
When the market breaks out of the Intersection, the old losers try to
maximize their profit. They take trades in the direction of the
breakout, which generates more movement.

@@b2203 h4
Supply-demand
equilibrium versus Nash equilibrium

@@b2204 p
The old supply-demand theory says the market always seeks an
equilibrium value between supply and demand. Buyers and sellers each
look for the equilibrium price, so the future price can be found from
that balance. That is what university teaches about the market:

@@b2205 blockquote
<p>"Why is the equilibrium price the best deal available for both buyers
and sellers? The equilibrium price reflects that the highest price
consumers are willing to pay for that amount of the good or service and
is just equal to the minimum price that suppliers require for delivering
it." — McConnell, Brue and Flynn, Introduction to Microeconomic
Principles, Chapter 3</p>

@@b2206 p
But the equilibrium price is not the best deal. In my opinion, the
market follows the Nash equilibrium for a simple reason: it is a very
competitive environment. In any competitive game, the Nash equilibrium
is the best choice for all players.

@@b2207 h3
The Components of the
Market Microscope

@@b2208 h4
Trend, maximum profit and
climax

@@b2209 p
Old market wisdom says the trend is "the path of least resistance",
and an experienced trader follows it: "the trend is your friend". It is
your friend because it puts the maximum gain in your pocket. But traders
follow it only until they take their maximum profit — the Nash
equilibrium.

@@b2210 p
That is why the market always looks for oversold and overbought
conditions:

@@b2211 ul
<li><p><strong>Oversold:</strong> the maximum point sellers can
achieve.</p></li>
<li><p><strong>Overbought:</strong> the maximum point buyers can
achieve.</p></li>

@@b2212 p
Adding this to my Energy model creates a new concept: each swing
measures the pulling force that drives it to its maximum. The impression
forces carry a lot of passion and emotion, pushed by the pulling force
toward maximum gain. The market is always looking for the Nash
equilibrium price.

@@b2213 p
What happens when the market reaches its Nash equilibrium (oversold
or overbought)? It retreats and corrects. The energy inside the pulling
force drives the impression force until it reaches maximum gain, and
then the energy is at climax.

@@b2214 h4
The battery model

@@b2215 p
It is exactly like a battery. The market charges the battery, and the
battery carries the trend until it runs out. When there is no battery
left, the trend shuts down and the next phase is correction — just like
a smartphone going from 100% to 0% and shutting down.

@@b2216 p
This is relative energy. When the swing reaches its Nash equilibrium,
traders have their maximum profit at that price. They take it, and that
is how a climax forms. Traders are happy, their passion is satisfied,
and the market moves into its correction phase.

@@b2217 p
Every swing has a maximum. By studying the old swings, we can measure
how energy responded in each one. Some phones run three days on one
charge, others seven. The battery's response depends on how the phone is
used and on the quality of the battery.

@@b2218 p
In the same way, every swing, up or down, shows its own energy and
response:

@@b2219 ul
<li><p>One swing moves 100 pips and its battery is at 0%.</p></li>
<li><p>Another swing moves 1,000 pips and its battery is still at
50%.</p></li>

@@b2220 p
By studying the quality of each swing and its response up to its
maximum, we can see on which side — up or down — the pulling and
impression forces are greater. It is like a simple test of two phones to
see which battery has more power.

@@b2222 p
Figure: The battery model of a swing: energy runs down to the climax.
A 100-pip swing at 0% against a 1,000-pip swing still at 50%.

@@b2223 h4
Shifting energy

@@b2224 p
How do we know the battery is finished in a swing? It is not
difficult. A phone's battery is finished when it cannot run an app, or
the app shuts down and cannot restart. In a swing, we compare it with
the old pulling force when it worked perfectly. If the difference is
large, there is no relative energy left.

@@b2225 p
I call this <strong>shifting energy</strong> in the swing. By
watching shifting energy, we can see which side has more pulling power
from the impression force to reach its maximum.

@@b2226 h4
How shifting energy
affects mass behavior

@@b2227 blockquote
<p>1. <strong>It changes mood and passion.</strong> When a phone battery
dies, the owner feels torn between sadness and anger. That energy pulls
him into action — he goes to find a charger.</p>
<p>2. <strong>It shows the path of least resistance.</strong> We see the
shift by comparing the maximum of each up and down swing. The side with
the greater pulling power and passion is the path of least resistance.
No one can fight real passion ("slave of the passions").</p>
<p>3. <strong>Passion shows in the response.</strong> Comparing the
upswings and downswings shows their response, which measures pulling
power and passion. As Steve Jobs said: "People don't know what they want
until you show it to them." When the market shows its offers, we see the
mass response to them.</p>
<p>4. <strong>The response is everything.</strong> Jack has $100 in his
pocket. A computer shop tries to sell him a $1,000 laptop, and he cannot
pay. A hundred meters on, a real-estate salesman wants to sell him a
$200,000 house. Can he sell it? Of course not — Jack could not even buy
the laptop. Anything can happen; maybe Jack gets a loan, as in 2008. But
the response tells us what to expect. This is "constant conjunction" —
induction.</p>

@@b2228 p
To summarize: the market is a salesman, and the mass shows its
passion for each item. Professional traders study that passion and
response, then trade with it — up for a positive mood, down for a
negative mood.
