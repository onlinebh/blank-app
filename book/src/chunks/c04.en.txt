@@b241 p
What makes the b-matrix gain the most while the others gain less? Any
movement, even in price, can be interpreted with Newton's first law of
motion: there must be a cause (a net external force) for any change in
velocity, either in magnitude or in direction.

@@b242 p
Velocity is V = Δx / Δt, where x is boxes and t is time. In words:
more boxes in less time means more velocity.

@@b243 p
The greatest buying velocity is in the b-matrix. But what made the
buyers so aggressive there, compared with the other Matrices? There are
two possible answers, and they are mutually exclusive events: {chance, a
definite cause}.

@@b244 h4
Two Kinds of Chance

@@b245 p
Let us discuss chance. There are two types:

@@b246 ul
<li><p><strong>Absolute chance</strong>: an effect without any cause.
Most philosophers do not accept this type. An example: someone throws a
coin and, as an effect, gains $1,000,000.</p></li>
<li><p><strong>Relative chance</strong>: an effect with a cause, but
without repetition. A coin flip has only two possibilities, {Tails,
Heads}. If someone keeps flipping, it cannot be that only Tails appear
in every 100 throws. No cause makes the coin show only Tails. Sometimes
Tails, sometimes Heads.</p></li>

@@b247 p
Another example: someone opens his door and suddenly finds his friend
in front of it. That is a chance with a cause, because his friend came
for some reason. But an event without repetition cannot become a rule.
We cannot say that every time he opens the door he will find his friend
there. It is a relative chance.

@@b248 h4
Relative Chance and the 3-C

@@b250 p
Figure: In case A the SPS achieves the 3-C count; in case B it
suddenly stops short — a relative chance that we must observe.

@@b251 p
At A the SPS achieves the 3-C count. At B, suddenly, the sellers
change their will or the buyers lose theirs. It is a relative chance.
Still, it is important to observe it when the SPS cannot achieve the
count, or when the SPS passes the over-count.

@@b252 p
So what is the probability that buying passes the over-count?

@@b253 p
Group = {buyers strong, buyers regular, buyers weak} = 3
possibilities. The probability = 1/3 = 33.3%.

@@b254 p
If there is no absolute chance in the world, and none in the market,
can we build a Matrix combination that removes "buyers weak" from the
group? Then the group is {buyers strong, buyers regular}, and the
probability = 1/2 = 50%.

@@b255 p
Everything — Propagation, Intersection, 3-C and the other advanced
Overlays — is an experiment that removes "buyers weak" from the group of
probabilities when we buy. Every Overlay is part of the whole; every
principle is part of the whole.

@@b256 p
Our job is to find the path where the opposite will is weakest, as in
the b-matrix. That lets us gain more profit.

@@b257 p
Remember: Goodman's theory is a holistic approach. Every concept is
part of the whole.

@@b258 h4
Balancing the Concepts

@@b259 p
Balance aims to bring all the concepts into one system of measures
that can eliminate the unwanted probability events. It does this through
either a joint or a disjoint relationship, which I explain below. We can
induce that balance from our own experiments:

@@b260 p
Probability of buying strong = (number of experiments showing strong
buying) / (total number of experiments, buying weak or strong).

@@b289 h3
1.3b The Cause Behind Each
Swing

@@b290 p
The market is a game of chances. The best way to show how price moves
is to imagine two players. One plays for the group on the buying side,
the other for the group on the selling side.

@@b291 p
Each has limited capital, but neither knows the other's capital. This
is the unknown factor. On each turn, each player decides only the lot
size. Lot = expectation, and both have free will to choose it. The
remainder is the amount left over after each turn.

@@b292 ul
<li><p><strong>Win rule</strong>: whoever puts in the larger lot
(expectation) wins after the remainder.</p></li>
<li><p><strong>Loss rule</strong>: whoever puts in the smaller lot
(expectation) loses after the remainder.</p></li>
<li><p><strong>Equal</strong>: equal lots leave no remainder, so no one
wins or loses.</p></li>

@@b294 p
The basic rule of the trend is to follow the remainder of the orders
coming from buyers and sellers. Watch for an unusual order, such as the
10,000 from the buying side. It shows that buyers have changed their
opinion by being willing to pay for large lots. It reveals their
expectation.

@@b295 p
In brief: lots = volume = expectation. The greater the variance in
expectation, the more it points to important buying or selling.
