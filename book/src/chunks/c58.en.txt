Bruce Gould
and His Father (2023-09-27, 2021-10-03)

@@b3536 p
Bruce Gould was, Michael told me, "one of the few people Charlie
respected." When a large fund manager went looking for traders around
1977, Michael recommended Bruce. Bruce said no:

@@b3537 blockquote
<p>"No, sorry, I am happy as is. I sit in quiet office, trade, talk
story with my elderly father who join me every day."</p>

@@b3538 p
Michael's comment: "So, he trade, learn good life lessons from father
same time. Perfect, no interest in fund. But I make friend with Bruce,
talk on-off through the years."

@@b3539 p
Bruce "taught trading as if it was a business," Michael wrote in
2021, and recommended his <em>World's Greatest Money Book</em>. Michael
himself wrote a long article, "Trading as a Business," while he was in
Hawaii.

@@b3540 p
<em>The lesson:</em> not every opportunity is an improvement. Bruce
already had what most traders chase the fund for: a quiet office, a
method that worked, and time with the people he loved. Knowing when you
have enough is part of money management.

@@b3541 h3
"Let's Go, Dad!" (2026-03-26)

@@b3542 p
Michael made his first commodity trade in September 1973, a few weeks
after meeting Charlie at Peavey. They had been tracking a double
intersection in silver on the daily chart, and a small setup formed on
their short-term point-and-figure charts. When the buy signal came,
"Charlie leaned over to me, 'Let's go, dad!'"

@@b3543 p
He bought two contracts at $2.78 an ounce. "We took one off at the
Under count around 3.30 and when it repropagated back to the Tipping
Point of $3.30/oz we re-established the Under position. Near $6.00/oz we
liquidated our positions." His verdict: "I was hooked on both
commodities and Mr. Goodman's amazing methodology! Plans to go back to
school for a Ph.D. in Philosophy were scrapped!"

@@b3544 p
<em>The lesson:</em> even on his first trade Michael took a profit at
the count and still came back for the bigger move when the market
repropagated. Bank the Under, then let the structure invite you back
in.

The Magic Dates (2025-11-22)

@@b3546 p
Around 1977 Michael saw a small ad in <em>Commodities</em> magazine
(later <em>Futures</em>) for the "Magic Dates Silver Trades." It cost
about five dollars. What arrived was a small laminated card with nine or
ten dates in the year to buy or sell silver: "Buy Dec 1, Sell Jan 2, Buy
Jan 15, Sell Mar 1..." To his surprise, "it actually held up fairly well
for a few years." Later he understood why the December-January dates
worked: until the government closed the door, silver was used for
year-end tax straddles. "Those days are gone."

@@b3547 p
<em>The lesson:</em> a calendar system can work for a while, but only
because of a hidden reason in the market. When the reason disappears, so
does the edge. Ask why a method works before you trust it.

@@b3548 h3
Kroll, Gould and the sitting
ducks

@@b3549 p
<em>Why the slower trader won.</em>

@@b3550 p
In about 1977, Michael read the letter of a trader named Bruce Gould
and was impressed. At about the same time John van Eck, who ran the big
International Investors gold fund, decided to start a commodity fund.
Such things were very new. He would give three traders $50,000 each, and
the best would manage the fund. One of Michael's brokers, a top salesman
for the firm, persuaded Van Eck to use Michael as one of the three.

@@b3551 p
Van Eck asked Michael if he knew anybody else. Michael recommended
Gould and asked him. Gould said no: "I am happy as is. I sit in a quiet
office, trade, and talk story with my elderly father who joins me every
day." He traded and learned life lessons from his father. Michael made a
friend for life. Charlie reminded him: "Just go for the high percentage
sitting ducks, Dad!"

@@b3552 p
Van Eck also chose Stanley Kroll, a very well-known trader with a big
mouth and a big reputation. In three weeks Michael made about 5% in two
trades. Kroll burned up his entire $50,000 and came back hat in hand to
ask for another try. Van Eck gave him another $50,000.

@@b3553 p
At the end of six months Kroll was up over 100%, trading in and out
like a madman. Michael had made about ten trades and was up 38%. Van
Eck, impressed by Kroll, gave him a fund with $1 million to start. Kroll
burned through it quickly, lost 50%, and the fund closed. Van Eck said
commodities were a bad deal and he would never try again. "I think the
Dutchman would understand risk, but not. Only saw reward."

@@b3554 p
<strong>What it means.</strong> A fast 100% looks better than a slow
38% until you ask what the person was risking to get it. Charlie
preferred the sitting ducks.

@@b3555 p
<strong>Try this.</strong> Rank your last twenty trades by risk
taken, not by profit. Notice which kind you would repeat.

@@b3556 h3
The
Sharpe Ratio That Was Wrong (2024-10-08, 2024-08-23)

@@b3557 p
Around 1986 a large New Jersey firm, Commodity Corporation, wanted to
look at Michael's trading program. He sent his track record and his own
calculation of the Sharpe Ratio, about 8.5. They called back: "Are you
sure on this Sharpe Ratio?? Cannot be right. We will refigure it." He
thought the opportunity was gone. Then they called again: "We
recalculate, you were wrong!" A pause. "It is actually 8.6!" He had
rounded a 5 down instead of up.

@@b3558 p
Then came the due diligence. "I spend two full days at hdqrs in New
Jersey... interviewed and grilled like suspect in murder, aahaha..."
They promised to monitor every trade against strict parameters. His very
first trade the next week, in the S&amp;P 500, was a large winner, and
the parameters were forgotten at once. His verdict: "Emotions trump due
diligence, always. Human nature."

@@b3559 p
<em>The lesson:</em> even professionals with every model and every
interview fall for one good trade. If the big firms cannot keep emotion
out of their decisions, the private trader must build his rules before
the excitement arrives.

@@b3560 h3
The Car Owns You (2023-11-15)

@@b3561 p
Since 1962 Michael had wanted a Jaguar XKE. In the 1970s he found one
like new and told Charlie he was going to buy it. Charlie said: "Dad,
you won't own the car, the car will own you!" Michael pointed out that
Charlie had a dozen or more Corvairs. "Yes, and I own THEM. I never
worry someone steal or hit."

@@b3562 p
About a year after Charlie was gone, Michael bought a nearly new
Ferrari. He drove it only on Sundays, on the back road to visit Jim
Bickford. But he parked near Jim's apartment and worried the whole time
that someone would hit it, steal it or scratch it. One day he told Jim
he had to go and check on the car. Jim said: "I think the car owns
you!!" Michael laughed, and he could almost hear Charlie chuckle: "I
told you so, Dad!"

@@b3563 p
<em>The lesson:</em> anything you must watch all the time does not
belong to you; you belong to it. A position too big to stop thinking
about is the same Ferrari.

@@b3564 h3
The Burning Cadillac
(2026-10-04)

@@b3565 p
"Nothing upset or perturbed Jim," Michael wrote. Around 1989 he
bought a client's family car, a perfectly kept 1979 Cadillac, and the
next evening he drove it to Boulder for dinner. On the way home, at
dusk, people on the street began waving. He waved back, thinking they
admired the car. The waving grew wilder; someone pointed behind him.
"There was a wall of flame behind us two blocks long!" The starter had
shorted, gas was leaking, and the car was burning. The fire department
came, then the tow truck, whose driver said: "I can't tow this car, it's
still on fire!" Back came the fire department.

@@b3566 p
Near eleven at night, badly shaken, Michael phoned Jim, who lived
four blocks away, and asked him to bring a few things from the store.
Jim, woken from sleep, only wondered how he would get to Golden with the
buses stopped. Fifteen minutes later he came walking down Arapahoe
Street with his backpack, handed over the bag, looked at the burnt-out
car and calmly asked if everyone was all right. Then he walked home.
"Never showed any shock or surprise, just sort of focused on the task at
hand and everything else was irrelevant! No superfluous conversation,
ever."

@@b3567 p
<em>The lesson:</em> when everything is on fire, the most valuable
person is the calm one who does the next useful thing. That is the
trader you want to be when a market explodes against you.
