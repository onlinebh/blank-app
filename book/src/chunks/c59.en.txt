The Random Walk at
Midnight (2023-10-24)

@@b3569 p
Around 1990 Michael bought soybeans. "LOTS of soybeans. Somewhere I
still have news tape say, 'large money manager buys 500 cars of
beans'... 'money manager continues to buy beans, 300 cars.'" He went to
bed. "Middle of the night I wake up in hot sweat think, 'What if market
really is only a random walk!!!' Ahaha."

@@b3570 p
<em>The lesson:</em> even a trader with a method, a record and
seventeen years of experience feels doubt when the position is large.
The cure is not to remove the doubt but to size the trade so that you
can sleep with it.

Kant and the Late Paper
(2024-11-17)

@@b3572 p
In college Michael once handed in a philosophy paper late. The rule
was strict: a late paper loses one grade. He went to the professor and
pleaded his case.

@@b3573 blockquote
<p>"He said, 'You know Kant's categorical imperative! No exceptions. If
you can find an exception to CI by Wednesday morning when I start
grading papers I will give you regular grade.' I rack my brain two days,
cannot find exceptions but several interesting ideas... I go in
Wednesday, admit cannot find exceptions but some interesting ideas! 'Go
ahead, I'm listening!' So I talk several minutes my ideas... He says,
'You really dug deep on CI! I'll go ahead and give you regular
grade.'"</p>

@@b3574 p
<em>The lesson:</em> he did not win by finding the answer. He won by
the honesty of saying he had not found it, and by the depth of the
search. Teachers, and markets, reward serious work more than clever
excuses.

@@b3575 h3
The Chess Exhibition
(2025-06-20)

@@b3576 p
Around 1980, Michael, a former university chess champion, visited a
coffee shop owned by an old college friend, with chessboards on every
table. The friend asked him to give a small simultaneous exhibition, one
player against several boards. Michael agreed to six or seven.

@@b3577 p
One opponent was said to be very strong. He played the Ruy Lopez
straight out of the book for ten or twelve moves. Then, suddenly, he
took his knight and put it back on its original square. Then the other
knight. Then a bishop, then the queen, then the other bishop, "soon,
only pawns out, no pieces." Michael advanced easily and gave checkmate a
few moves later. The man jumped up, and Michael thought he was about to
be hit. Instead he said: "You are only player who has been able to beat
my strategy!" and walked away.

@@b3578 p
<em>The lesson:</em> a memorized opening is not a method. The man
knew twelve moves of theory and nothing about why they were played, so
he invented a "strategy" that undid them. Many traders do the same with
a pattern copied from a book. Without understanding, they eventually
pull every piece back home.

Write It or Lose It
(2023-05-15)

@@b3580 p
In college Michael carried small pocket-size spiral notebooks.
"Whenever have thought, stop and write it in the book. I soon number
subjects - think eventually 17 - like 17.1, 17.2 and put date start on
cover of each book." By graduation he had well over a hundred of them,
and the summer before he began trading he typed them all out and carried
the pages through every move for years.

@@b3581 p
Then, back from Hawaii, the family's young puppy found the box while
no one was home. "Chew half of the pages, pee on other half, haha, dog
say 'this is what I think of your philosophy!' I salvaged about 15 pages
is all."

@@b3582 p
<em>The lesson:</em> write your ideas down the moment they come,
number them, date them, and keep a copy somewhere safer than a cardboard
box. A trading journal is the same discipline. "Write it or lose
it."

Jim
Bickford and Jonathan's Wave (2026-10-02, 2025-07-15)

@@b3584 p
"I met Jim in 1966 at Denver chess club. NO ONE, I mean NO ONE more
loyal friend than Jim!"

@@b3585 p
In the summer of 1984 Michael came back from Hawaii with the design
for a trading program he called Jonathan's Wave: "multiple indicators
talk to each other in language of ME... I thought brilliant at the
time." He asked Jim if he could program it. Jim was never one for
compliments, "just all facts, focus." He looked and said, "This looks
like it could actually work," which from Jim was a big compliment. "But
I can't do it in BASIC."

@@b3586 p
Michael bought a full Smalltalk package for the research. Jim learned
Smalltalk in less than a week, then said, "I don't think this will work
either." So Michael bought an early Prolog. "Jim learn it in less than a
week... write entire program bug free == two weeks!... Program run
almost six years, returns are well documented!"

@@b3587 p
There were other Jim stories. Once the two of them lost the
initialization string for an old modem; Jim started looking at 6 pm and
found it around 4 am. Around 2002 a broker experimented with "box
options": draw a box, and if price hits it, you win. "Jim play around,
discover big hole in algorithm... We go in hit, hit, hit ahahaha... then
they jiggle algorithm... Jim figure out again, hit, hit, hit... Third
time, same thing! LOL! Next time we go in, no more Box Options!!!"

@@b3588 p
Michael used to drive the side road from Denver to Boulder every
Sunday morning to meet Jim. "Brings back good memories of when Jim
Bickford and I explored a lot of ideas."

@@b3589 p
<em>The lesson:</em> a good idea needs a good partner. Michael had
the design; Jim had the patience to learn whatever language the idea
required. And loyalty, more than talent, is what Michael remembered
first.

The English Horn (2025-06-12)

@@b3591 p
Around 2003 Michael answered a newspaper ad for an English horn, an
instrument he wanted to learn at home. The seller was a widow in a small
house in an old part of Denver, selling belongings to pay the bills; the
horn had belonged to her husband's brother, a professional musician. He
paid $500. At a music shop the man looked it over: a top-of-the-line
Loree, almost new. "I don't buy these but I think you could get $7k-$8k
easily."

@@b3592 p
"I tell him I'll sell the horn as is, buy something inexpensive...
and pocket several thousand dollars." Then, driving home: "IT HITS ME -
The Oboe Story with Walter!!!" It was Charlie's oboe story, told earlier
in this book, from twenty-five years before. Michael took the horn back
to the woman and connected her with a buyer. She received $7,000,
returned his $500 and was very happy. "Talk about deja vu!!!!"

@@b3593 p
<em>The lesson:</em> a lesson is only learned when you obey it
against your own interest. Michael heard the oboe story in the 1970s; a
quarter of a century later it cost him several thousand dollars, and he
paid it gladly.

The Borrowed Picture
(2025-06-18)

@@b3595 p
Michael owned a web conferencing company from 1998 to 2010. Around
2005 he hired an outside team to refresh its website. "They do nice work
- home page has beautiful, relevant image!!! ... everyone compliment me
on new look." Soon a courier brought a letter: the image was
copyrighted, and a Canadian company demanded payment or it would sue. He
hired an internet lawyer, took the image down and settled. Later he
learned that removing it on notice would probably have been enough. "So,
$7k + $3k for attorney down toilet."

@@b3596 p
<em>The lesson:</em> check where your material comes from before you
publish it, and get good advice before you pay, not after. Both are
cheap compared with skipping them.

Bet on the Method
(2025-07-22)

@@b3598 p
Around 2010 a good friend of Michael's, a keen Go player, argued that
computers had beaten chess but would need decades to beat Go, because
the game is so much larger. Michael had been in touch with a researcher
in the UK combining heuristic algorithms with Monte Carlo sampling. "I
told my friend 'less than ten years' Go lose to computers." In 2016
AlphaGo beat the world champion Lee Sedol with exactly that kind of
search plus sampling.

@@b3599 p
<em>The lesson:</em> do not judge the future by the size of the
problem; judge it by the method being applied to it. Michael read the
method, not the headlines.

@@b3600 h3
Watch for Blue Hondas
(2023-09-27)

@@b3601 p
Michael taught his students that seeing needs a hypothesis. He
borrowed Charlie's line about the traders in the Peavey gallery, "They
look but do not see," and added an exercise of his own: "You drive to
next city, call me. I ask, 'How many blue Hondas you see?' No idea. Tell
them, 'Watch for blue Hondas, then can know!'"

@@b3602 p
<em>The lesson:</em> you find on a chart what you have decided to
look for. Know the setup you want before you open the screen, or a
hundred setups will drive past unseen.
