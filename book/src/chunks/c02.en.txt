@@b114 h2
1. Twelve hours in the field

@@b115 p
<em>A mind trained by long, quiet work.</em>

@@b116 p
Charles B. Goodman was born on 23 October 1916 and grew up in Eads,
Colorado, where his family farmed corn and, Michael believes, rye
through the dust-bowl years. He told Michael he once planted three times
in a single season before a crop took hold. As a teenager he spent
twelve to fourteen hours a day in the field. "He had a lot of time to
think about life," Michael wrote.

@@b117 p
To trade, he drove to the nearest brokerage house, in Garden City,
Kansas, beside the railroad tracks. In the 1930s that meant a long trip
on unkept dirt roads. "On the drive he had a lot of time to think about
the markets!"

@@b118 p
He once told Michael that when the sun was hot and he felt he was
dying after ten hours of work, he did not think of pleasant things. He
thought of the world and of mankind, and asked why this and why that.
"It keeps the pain away and I keep working."

@@b119 p
<strong>What it means.</strong> Charlie's method came from hours of
slow attention, not from a screen. Depth comes from time spent with one
question.

@@b120 p
<strong>Try this.</strong> Pick one chart and sit with it for twenty
minutes without trading. Write one question about what you see.

@@b121 h2
2. "Listen to every damn word"

@@b122 p
<em>How a 23-year-old was chosen.</em>

@@b123 p
Michael met Charlie in July 1973 at the Peavey &amp; Company office
in Denver. Charlie had quit trading professionally. "I've made mine," he
said. But he loved the act of trading: studying charts and comparing the
markets with life.

@@b124 p
The office was crowded with traders, and Charlie rarely spoke to new
ones. Whenever he talked over one of the extra-large charts he kept at
the office, people gathered around to catch a small morsel of his
wisdom. When the manager, Pete Rednor, saw Charlie sharing his secrets
with the new kid, he advised Michael: "Listen to every damn word he
says!"

@@b125 p
Why Charlie chose Michael, Michael says he will never really
know.

@@b126 p
<strong>What it means.</strong> The best lessons are often offered to
the person who shows up and keeps showing up.

@@b127 p
<strong>Try this.</strong> Find one person whose work you respect and
ask one good question this week. Then listen.

@@b128 h2
3. The cat on the doorstep

@@b129 p
<em>The only time Michael saw him so sad.</em>

@@b130 p
Charlie was not fond of pets. When Michael once brought his dog to
the house, Charlie grew nervous, and the dog had to wait in the
bedroom.

@@b131 p
Then one day he came home and found an injured stray cat on his
doorstep, bleeding after a fight. He wrapped it in a towel and took it
to the vet. The vet stitched it up and gave medicine, but would not keep
the cat. Charlie tried to give it away. No one wanted it. Reluctantly,
he kept it.

@@b132 p
Soon they were good friends. The cat rode in Charlie's car between
Eads and Denver. After about a year the old cat fell sick and had to be
put down. The next day, when Charlie told Michael what had happened,
Michael saw him wipe tears from his eyes. "I think it was the only time
I saw him so sad."

@@b133 p
<strong>What it means.</strong> The man who could read a market in
silence was gentle with a hurt animal. Calm in the markets and kindness
at home came from the same character.

@@b134 p
<strong>Try this.</strong> After your next hard trade, do one small
kind thing before you open another chart.

@@b135 h2
4. The house on Colfax and
Fillmore

@@b136 p
<em>A roll-top desk, a window, and a library of mimeographed
secrets.</em>

@@b137 p
Charlie's Denver house stood on the corner of Colfax and Fillmore.
His huge roll-top desk faced a large window onto Fillmore Street, where
he usually kept two or three of his Corvairs parked. The other house, in
Eads, had three stories and about six thousand square feet. Michael
remembers it as beautifully furnished.

@@b138 p
Charlie collected privately published system books and monographs
from the 1930s to the 1960s. Some were proper books and some were ten or
fifteen mimeographed pages, printed in runs of fifty to two hundred
copies. His favorite was a two-volume spiral-bound work on point and
figure charting in dark red faux-leather covers. He had paid $500 for it
in 1948 and reminded Michael more than once to be careful with it.

@@b139 p
"Dad, these are yours when I go to the Spirit World," he told
Michael. After Charlie's death someone else drove from Denver to Eads
and took them. Michael still looks for that set online.

@@b140 p
<strong>What it means.</strong> Charlie valued study. He paid $500
for two volumes of charts in 1948 and lent them like a treasure.

@@b141 p
<strong>Try this.</strong> Choose one book or paper you have never
finished and read ten pages today.

Introduction
to Goodman: Method, Probability and the Will of the Market (2022)

@@b143 p
In this introduction I want to lay the ground before explaining
Goodman's theory itself. I start with the scientific method and with
probability, because a trader needs both. Then I show how human behavior
in markets formed, how it repeats, and how Goodman Wave Theory reads
that behavior bar by bar, swing by swing and Matrix by Matrix. I finish
with chance, balance, anticipation and managing the trade.

@@b144 h3
The Scientific Method and
the Trader

@@b145 p
The kindness of scientists is a favor to every one of us. Because of
them, the path to success becomes easier. Their efforts make the
impossible possible.

@@b146 p
A scientist has the ability to take a critical problem from
complexity to simplicity. He encourages people to learn and to do the
right thing at the right time.

@@b147 p
The history of science is the story of the difference between dark
and light, between confusion and evidence. It is the story of
experiments and of observing phenomena. Scientists collect evidence by
day and in the dark of night, day after day and year after year. They
continue until the hypothesis is proven. Then the theory becomes clear
and easy to follow, and anyone can pick its fruit.

@@b148 p
In brief, the scientific method has three steps:

@@b149 blockquote
<p>1. Ask a research question and make a hypothesis.</p>
<p>2. Collect the evidence, either through an experiment or by observing
the phenomena.</p>
<p>3. Analyze the results and conclude whether they confirm or disprove
the hypothesis.</p>

@@b150 p
These three steps are the important ones. The path to success in
trading opens for the one who is willing to experiment and practice the
theory, as Mr. Goodman did with his many "bathtubs". Those who do not
want to exercise the theory fool themselves. They will never take a
single step on this path. There is no shortcut.

@@b151 p
Our first job is to practice these steps. Without action there is no
result, and every action brings a result. Good or bad, the result helps
us. The result is the best teacher.

@@b152 blockquote
<p>"I make it by my hand, I visualize it in my head, I collect the
evidence, I test it, and never fail."</p>
