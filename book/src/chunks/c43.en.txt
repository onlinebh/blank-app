Poka-Yoke: make the error
impossible

@@b2759 p
Poka-Yoke is a Japanese engineering principle. It means
mistake-proofing: a mechanism in a process that prevents, corrects, or
draws attention to a human error as it happens. A plug that fits only
one way is Poka-Yoke. Nobody needs discipline to plug it in
correctly.

@@b2760 p
On 2026-09-17 I told Michael I had used the idea for years and
finally turned it into a trading expert for MetaTrader 5:

@@b2761 blockquote
<p>"I converted it into a trading expert that almost guarantees what
most traders fail to achieve: letting me trade easily, focused only on
the setup, with less thinking on "money"."</p>

@@b2762 p
The idea behind it is that most traders do not fail from poor
analysis. They fail from one disastrous event: a trade without a stop,
revenge after a loss, overconfidence after a streak, doubling down to
recover. So the expert does not touch the analysis. It guards the
failure modes.

@@b2764 p
Figure: The twelve protection layers of the Poka-Yoke Guardian, each
one a guard against a named failure mode, grouped by where the error
comes from: size, mind, method, market and review.

@@b2765 h3
The twelve layers

@@b2766 p
The layers fall into five groups.

@@b2767 p
<strong>Size.</strong> Layer 1 calculates the lot from the real stop
distance, which I set by dragging a line on the chart, so there is no
guessing. Layer 2 fixes the maximum drawdown for a 30-trade cycle and
locks it until the campaign ends. This is my Thirty Souls rule, made
into a lock. Layer 3 watches floating equity and closes everything if
the daily limit is breached. Layer 4 is anti-Martingale: after losing
days the size steps down, 100% to 75% to 50% to 25% and then a full
stop, and it returns to 100% after a winning day. Layer 8 caps the total
risk open across all trades.

@@b2768 p
<strong>Mind.</strong> Layer 5 is a cooling-off period: after a loss,
no new trade on the same pair for a set time. Revenge trading simply
cannot happen. Layer 7 will not execute a trade until I write a clear
reason and record my state: calm, excited, frustrated or tired. Layer 11
asks for extra confirmation after a series of wins, because
overconfidence is the 9 on my emotion scale.

@@b2769 p
<strong>Method.</strong> Layer 6 is the Propagation Lens in five
stages: Propagation, Intersection, 3-C Count, Overlays, Dagger. The
stages cannot be skipped, and cancelling one invalidates all the stages
after it. This is the 1-2-3 of my reading, enforced.

@@b2770 p
<strong>Market.</strong> Layer 9 is the Correlated Exposure Barrier,
which stops two trades on the same currency. Layer 10 filters
high-impact news before and after the event.

@@b2771 p
<strong>Review.</strong> Layer 12 calculates the Golden Ratios every
10 and 30 trades and keeps a permanent log, with chart snapshots at
every entry and exit.

@@b2772 p
Every layer follows one principle: <strong>"When in doubt,
block—don't allow."</strong> If the news filter cannot verify the
calendar, it stops trading rather than assuming it is safe.

@@b2773 h3
What it cannot do

@@b2774 p
I was honest with Michael about the limits. "The system
<strong>manages</strong> your analytical edge; it does not create one."
If the analysis is weak, it slows the bleeding but does not stop it.
Fixed size grows slower than compounding, and that is a choice, not a
flaw. And it depends on my honesty when I type my reason and my state.
It stops automatic rule-breaking, not deliberate lies.

@@b2775 p
I wrote that it is "good for beginners and those who have lack
discipline." I would add: it is good for experienced traders on their
bad days. Fifty years did not make me immune to a 9 on the scale.

15. The oboe

@@b2781 p
<em>A story Michael calls long, with a short lesson.</em>

@@b2782 p
Walter was a Vietnam veteran in poor shape. Michael believes he had
been close to a landmine when it exploded. He had many mental problems
and was slow to think and to move. He had once been considered a top
oboe scholarship prospect, but chose the Army because his father had
been a military man.

@@b2783 p
Charlie suggested Walter take up the oboe again, mostly as therapy.
Double-reed instruments are not cheap, so Charlie gave him about two
hundred dollars to find a decent used one.

@@b2784 p
A few days later Walter came back with an oboe, and for him, because
his emotions were very muted, he was excited. He had answered a
newspaper ad from an old woman who lived alone. The oboe was a
professional-quality instrument worth several thousand dollars. His plan
was to sell it, buy a cheaper one and pocket two or three thousand.

@@b2785 p
Charlie questioned him. The woman was very poor. Her husband had been
a professional musician, and she clearly suffered from some form of
dementia. Charlie explained that Walter could sell the oboe, buy an
inexpensive one, and take the rest of the money back to her. "Oh, no,
Charlie! I can do a lot with the money."

@@b2786 p
Charlie told him he would never take such money. "The longer you
wait, the worse things will get, Walter. At some point the universe has
to re-establish a balance by punishing you. And it charges interest.
Something might happen to you years from now and you'll have no idea
why, but it will be for taking advantage of an old woman years before.
There are infinite connections among things in this world and we should
not disturb them if we can avoid it."

@@b2787 p
Walter did not listen. He sold the oboe, bought a cheap one and kept
the money. In the next few weeks, as Michael remembers: the cheap oboe
had a crack in the body and was worthless; he got a flat tire on the
highway; the doctor he liked at the military hospital moved away and the
new doctor would not give him a medicine he needed; and a pipe broke and
flooded his small apartment. Each time something happened, Charlie said,
"Take the money back to the old woman, Walter."

@@b2788 p
Finally Walter decided to return the money, but admitted he had spent
some. Charlie gave him the difference. He took the money back. On the
way home he stopped for something to eat and met a really nice
girlfriend, who helped him with the things he found difficult.

@@b2789 p
Charlie reminded him that if he had never returned it, it would have
been a black cloud over his life, and some day it would rain very hard
on him.

@@b2790 p
<strong>What it means.</strong> A wrong you leave unrepaired grows.
Charlie's rule was practical: pay it back early, while it is small.

@@b2791 p
<strong>Try this.</strong> Think of one thing you owe someone, a
reply, an apology, a few dollars. Settle it today.

16. Never think about
getting paid back

@@b2793 p
<em>The Corvair that went to a young mother.</em>

@@b2794 p
One morning Charlie called Michael and asked for a lift to the bus
station. Michael joked: "They finally caught up with you and you have to
leave town!" Charlie laughed. He only wanted to take the bus down to
Eads.

@@b2795 p
Two days later he called again. When Michael arrived, Charlie had
brought another Corvair to Denver and was washing it. "What's the deal?"
"I have a gal who needs a car to go to a new job and take her baby to
day care, so I'm giving her this car."

@@b2796 p
Michael said, "That's nice of you, Charlie! You'll get paid back for
the good deed."

@@b2797 p
"Dad, when you do something for someone you NEVER think about getting
paid back. You do it because it is the right thing to do. Thinking you
will be rewarded spoils the gift!"

@@b2798 p
<strong>What it means.</strong> A gift with a hidden invoice is a
trade. Charlie wanted to be clear about which one he was making.

@@b2799 p
<strong>Try this.</strong> Do one favor this week that no one will
ever know about.

17. The fifty-dollar
investment

@@b2801 p
<em>Charlie at the dog track, explained like Socrates.</em>

@@b2802 p
Charlie liked to play the dog races for relaxation. Sometimes he and
Michael went every night for a week or two. During one of those
stretches a younger man at the track befriended Charlie. Michael did not
like the man but said nothing. After several nights the man asked
Charlie to lend him $50, which he would pay back a little at a time
whenever they met.

@@b2803 p
Michael was surprised to see Charlie hand it over. It was fairly
clear that the man had been setting Charlie up. Of course they never saw
him again.

@@b2804 p
"Didn't you know he was just setting you up?" Michael asked.

@@b2805 p
"Of course I did. He invested a lot of energy over several days to
get that job done. It was well planned. Because he expended so much
energy, the misdeed of stealing $50 will stick to him like glue until he
does some act to undo it. It's different than if he just picked
somebody's pocket on the spur of the moment. He won't be able to shake
loose of it. Either he will die with it or it will turn his life around
eventually. So I invested $50 in him."

@@b2806 p
Michael adds that after fifty years he cannot give the words
verbatim, but they are close.

@@b2807 p
<strong>What it means.</strong> Charlie treated a theft as an
investment in another person's conscience. It cost him $50 and did not
cost him his peace.

@@b2808 p
<strong>Try this.</strong> The next time someone wrongs you in a
small way, ask what the wrong will cost <em>them</em> before you ask
what it cost you.
