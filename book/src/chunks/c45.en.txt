21. Relaxed and excited at
the same time

@@b2891 p
<em>Charlie on love, and on trading.</em>

@@b2892 p
Around 1978, when Michael was a young man still unsure about the girl
he was seeing, one day he and Charlie were at Charlie's Denver house
looking at charts. "Well, Dad, is this the one? Are you in love with
her?" Michael said he did not know. How do you know you're in love? He
asked it rhetorically, but he saw Charlie's eyes go still and look off
into space, and he knew a lecture was coming.

@@b2893 p
"I dated four women before I met June. I was in my twenties, like
you. I had a little more money than most guys my age, like you. The
difference, Dad, is I wasn't spending it like water pouring out of an
open drain!" He smiled and chuckled. Charlie's laugh was disarming. He
could tell you that you were the world's biggest fool and laugh, and you
would not be offended.

@@b2894 p
"I felt different about June and asked myself why. I thought of the
four other women. Two of them I felt very comfortable with, like I'd
known them for years. I felt at ease, I could be myself and I didn't
have to worry about how I said something, how I looked, how I moved. But
there was no real excitement. In a way, it was like dating your
sister.

@@b2895 p
"The other two women, I felt very excited when I saw them. Very
upbeat, almost a King Kong feeling, that I could accomplish anything,
that life was good and people were good. But I really wasn't comfortable
with them. I felt I had to think before I spoke, how did I look, did I
do this right, did I do that right.

@@b2896 p
"It hit me! With June, somehow those two seemingly mutually-exclusive
emotions were folded into a single emotion. I felt enormously
comfortable and at ease with her. In fact I was sure I had known her in
another life. But she also excited me and made me feel happy in a way
I'd never felt even with the other two women. I concluded I was in
love." June left him for another man soon after. "I've never met another
woman who made me feel like she did, relaxed and excited at the same
time."

@@b2897 p
He went quiet and gazed away, so Michael kept quiet too, knowing more
was coming.

@@b2898 p
"Dad, trading is like that. You have to be in love with it, both
relaxed and excited at the same time. Just relaxed and you'll miss good
trades. Just excited and you'll do even worse and make bad trades. It
has to be both in you at the same time!"

@@b2899 p
Years later Michael used Charlie's test himself. "It fit how I felt
perfectly."

@@b2900 p
Michael added later that Charlie often stressed an emotional sweet
spot you had to be in to succeed: focused but loose, avoiding risk but
not afraid of it.

@@b2901 p
<strong>What it means.</strong> Calm without excitement misses
trades, and excitement without calm makes bad ones. Charlie asked for
both at once.

@@b2902 p
<strong>Try this.</strong> Before you enter, rate yourself from 1 to
5 for calm and 1 to 5 for excitement. Trade only when both are at least
3.

22. The raccoon

@@b2904 p
<em>The only time Charlie called him Michael.</em>

@@b2905 p
There was a park three or four blocks from Charlie's Denver house,
half wooded and half open, with a walking trail. To reach it you had to
cross a very busy street. Charlie did not like to walk any further than
he needed, because his feet were in horrible shape from fieldwork. But a
few times, perhaps five between 1973 and 1979, he said, "Dad, let's go
to the park. My mind is too full and I need to let it clear up some
before we look at charts."

@@b2906 p
One day, as they walked down the busy street, a small raccoon
streaked out of the bushes and ran across the road toward the park. A
car going about ten miles over the limit hit it just as it dove for the
curb. It spun and landed at the edge of the park by some bushes.

@@b2907 p
Charlie stopped. "Michael, I hope he died instantly." It was one of
only two or three times he called Michael by his name instead of
"Dad."

@@b2908 p
They crossed the street. The raccoon was dead, its mouth wide open,
almost like a smile. Michael looked at Charlie, but Charlie looked away,
pulled a handkerchief from his pocket and wiped his eyes. "A man
weeping," Michael wrote.

@@b2909 p
They walked into the park and said nothing for several minutes. Then
Charlie spoke for a while. Michael cannot quote him exactly, but this
was the point:

@@b2910 p
"Dad, close only counts in horseshoes. In life you are either right
or wrong. Sometimes being wrong is not too bad for you: you fail a test
in school, you have a bad job interview. But sometimes being wrong can
be fatal, like it was for that little raccoon. If the driver had been
going five miles slower, the raccoon would have made it to the park, to
paradise: woods, a stream, people leave food, maybe he finds a little
girl raccoon. But not. We must do our best, but know that fate is always
in charge of our life."

@@b2911 p
Charlie rarely got emotional. It was very difficult for Michael to
see. They went back to Charlie's house, skipped the charts, and Michael
went home.

@@b2912 p
<strong>What it means.</strong> Do your best, and do not confuse your
best with control. Both halves are true.

@@b2913 p
<strong>Try this.</strong> After a loss you could not have prevented,
write down what you did right. Then close the platform for the day.
