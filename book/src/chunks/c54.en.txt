What the series taught me

@@b3382 p
Figure: The series on a time axis, from Study 1 in late October 2021
to Study 28 in January 2022, with the theme of each study in one

@@b3383 p
Writing a study each week taught me that the market is a movie, not a
photograph. In Study 4 I expected the bullish cycle; in Study 5 buyers
withdrew, and I had to read the new scene honestly. The method does not
protect us from surprise. It shows us, step by step, who is losing
energy.

@@b3384 p
The most reliable message came from volume read against progress.
Sellers pushing on falling volume, buyers absorbing offers like a
sponge, a Test line that holds: these told me more than any single bar.
Don't look at bars; look at lines and Propagations.

@@b3385 p
I learned to use two timeframes as an Overlay. A Goldilocks or a
Dagger on the 15-minute or 1-hour chart only mattered when it could
propagate into the larger Matrix. Without that Overlay, as in Study 13,
I gave the trade no more than 50%.

@@b3386 p
The key levels kept returning: the Excess and Test lines, the Return
Zone, the TP and the SS. Each is a place where one side must prove
itself.

@@b3387 p
Above all, the series taught patience and introspection. Ask: what do
I see now, and what do I want to see next? As Mr. Goodman said, "You do
not need to predict prices to make money."

The Forex Bullish
Consensus Reports (2022–2023)

@@b3389 p
In 2022 Michael and I wrote a short booklet together: <em>Using
Contrary Opinion and Bullish Consensus in the FOREX Markets — A new and
unique trading edge for currency traders</em>. It explains the method
behind the FOREX Bullish Consensus Report (FBCR), which we then ran week
after week. This chapter sets out that method, and then shows what the
weekly reports actually recorded.

@@b3390 h3
Part one: the method

@@b3391 h4
What the FOREX market lacks

@@b3392 p
Compared with equities and futures, FOREX has clear advantages.
Liquidity is much better than in futures. Leverage is many times higher
than in futures, even with the CFTC's constraints on margin.

@@b3393 p
Its single biggest weakness is the lack of a central exchange. Next
to the futures markets, three types of information are missing:

@@b3394 ul
<li><p><strong>Volume.</strong> Each FOREX broker aggregates volume from
its own liquidity providers. This does not reflect total volume across
the FX world. Adding up several platforms does not help either, because
one liquidity provider often serves many platforms, so the data stays
skewed.</p></li>
<li><p><strong>Open interest.</strong> Open interest counts the
contracts outstanding. It rises when a new buyer and a new seller enter,
falls when an existing buyer liquidates to an existing seller, and stays
the same when a new party replaces an existing one. This needs a central
exchange, which FOREX does not have.</p></li>
<li><p><strong>Hedgers versus speculators.</strong> Futures markets
publish the Commitment of Traders (CoT) report, which splits
participants into large and small speculators and hedgers. Used
properly, it is a valuable predictive tool. FX has nothing like
it.</p></li>

@@b3395 p
Michael, a futures trader since 1973, has always found open interest
worth studying as a predictive indicator. He worked for a long time on a
"synthetic open interest" for currencies, built from price, range and
other factors. In his words: "Currently close, but at this writing no
cigar!"

@@b3396 p
Some years ago his team wrote a program called Mercury Charts. It
shows a standard daily OHLC bar chart with one enhancement: the colour
of each bar carries two extra pieces of information. The upper half
shows whether volume rose or fell from the previous day. The lower half
shows whether open interest rose or fell.

@@b3397 p
In most cases, aggregating information in FOREX does not work. But
there is one wonderful exception that gives FOREX traders real
predictive information: trader opinion.

@@b3398 h4
Contrary opinion and
discounting

@@b3399 p
Every seasoned trader knows the adage: "Buy on the rumor, sell on the
news." It is an example of contrary opinion and of the market process
called discounting.

@@b3400 p
Michael tells an old story from the 1970s futures markets. For
decades there had been a "February Bounce" in wheat futures, tied to the
winter wheat crop. By the time he arrived in 1973, so many traders knew
about it, and bought in advance, that the bounce was gone. On several
occasions there was a February <em>break</em> instead, as the early
buyers took profits.

@@b3401 p
The logic is simple:

@@b3402 blockquote
<p>1. Enough traders anticipate an event and buy or sell before it
happens.</p>
<p>2. When the event arrives, there is no buying power or selling
pressure left to move the market much further.</p>
<p>3. The event may even push the market in the opposite direction.</p>

@@b3403 p
This is discounting. As Charles B. Goodman put it: "To many traders
making too much money too quickly-Watch Out!"

@@b3404 p
The classic bar chart patterns show the same thing. So many traders
learned the Head and Shoulders pattern that the right shoulder grew
weaker and weaker from anticipated buying or selling. One small tip: in
today's world of automated trading and "expert advisors", the old bar
chart formations may again be worth a look.

@@b3405 p
"If you use the techniques and methods of the majority – you will
fail like the majority." A corollary is to use techniques and methods
that are not popular with the majority.

@@b3406 p
Contrary opinion, going against the crowd, works in much the same way
as discounting. When a large majority of traders are bullish or bearish,
at some point there is not enough buying power or selling pressure left
to push the market further. For a short time an imbalance of buyers or
sellers exists. The market then moves, sometimes sharply, in the
opposite direction to correct it.

@@b3407 h4
Bullish consensus:
measuring contrary opinion

@@b3408 p
Contrary opinion is the phenomenon. Bullish consensus is the
technique that measures it, so it can be analyzed, studied and used to
predict.

@@b3409 p
The consensus belongs to a defined group of traders. It is the
percentage of traders in that pool who are bullish.

@@b3410 p
Earl Hadady did the seminal work on contrary opinion and bullish
consensus in commodity futures in the 1970s. His book is worth reading;
the latest edition is <em>Contrary Opinion: Using Sentiment to Chart the
Markets</em> (John Wiley and Sons, 2000). His rule works like this, all
else being equal:

@@b3411 ul
<li><p><strong>Above 90% bullish: the market is a Sell.</strong> You go
against the crowd.</p></li>
<li><p><strong>Below 20% bullish: the market is a Buy.</strong></p></li>

@@b3412 p
Why 20 and not the mirror image of 10? Because it takes more to turn
a bull market down than to turn a bear market up. These were Hadady's
original numbers. As a tribute to his ground-breaking work, we believe
they remain valid and the most accurate half a century later.

@@b3413 p
Bullish consensus is naturally systems-neutral. It can be added to
any timing indicator or method, and it complements every trader's
toolkit.

@@b3414 h4
The Triad: consensus
plus a timing tool

@@b3415 p
When Michael first traded futures at Peavey &amp; Company (later
acquired by Geldermann, and then by the later defunct Refco), he met one
of the few successful traders there, an elderly gentleman. This trader
used a very simple system that blended a moving average, a
relative-strength-type indicator and Hadady's bullish consensus numbers.
He called it the Triad.

@@b3416 p
It can be used in two ways:

@@b3417 blockquote
<p>1. <strong>Consensus as a filter.</strong> The bullish consensus acts
as a secondary tool that removes false signals from common
indicators.</p>
<p>2. <strong>Consensus as the primary tool.</strong> A common indicator
then helps time the proper entry.</p>

@@b3418 p
Bullish consensus works best together with a simple timing tool.
Michael rebuilt the Triad with a rule base, and it was published weekly
in the FOREX Bullish Consensus Report.

@@b3419 p
Remember: even when bullish consensus is your primary indicator, you
still rely on a timing tool, such as a moving average, for entry. A
market, especially a bull market, can stay above 90% while prices keep
rising. Readings can stay above 90% or below 20% for a short time, and
in a bull market two or three days can mean a significant price
change.

@@b3420 p
The original booklet showed examples of bullish consensus filtering a
moving average and a relative strength indicator on currency pairs. In
those examples the consensus helped avoid false signals. Divergences
between the consensus and prices or indicators often gave excellent
entry points.

@@b3421 h4
Where the opinions come from

@@b3422 p
You could scour the internet for free advice on currency trends, or
subscribe to many market gurus. Both are time consuming and expensive.
Neither gives clear opinions on specific pairs over a specific period,
so neither works well as a predictive tool.

@@b3423 p
Over his half-century in commodity futures, currencies and equities,
Michael built and nurtured a very large network of traders, professional
and amateur. The FBCR draws its figures from that network. Every Friday
it asks one question:

@@b3424 blockquote
<p>"Are you bullish or bearish on [Currency Pair] for the next week?" If
no opinion, please skip this pair.</p>

@@b3425 p
There are two separate pools:

@@b3426 ul
<li><p><strong>Professionals.</strong> This is the group most followed:
money managers, bank and corporate traders, and individuals who trade
larger amounts. They are less swayed by news reports and other outside
influences. So when most of them agree, and the numbers are very high or
very low, "things happen." Each Friday 45–50 professionals are polled on
the eighteen most active currency pairs.</p></li>
<li><p><strong>Amateurs.</strong> Michael's original FBC, published by
FXstreet.com some years ago, reported professionals only. Amateur
behaviour is more erratic, but the basic 90/20 numbers still apply and
often prove insightful. Each Friday 55–60 amateurs are polled on the
same eighteen pairs.</p></li>

@@b3427 h4
Torturing the data

@@b3428 p
There is much more in the raw bullish consensus than the 90/20 rule.
The late James L. Bickford, a mathematics and statistics genius Michael
worked with for nearly 40 years, used to say: "You can torture the data,
but can you make it talk?"
