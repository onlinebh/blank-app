@@b795 h2
10. Silver and soybeans

@@b796 p
<em>Why two unrelated markets moved together.</em>

@@b797 p
Michael was watching the markets at Peavey and noticed that every
time silver rallied or fell sharply, soybeans followed. He worked out a
wild theory to explain why and told Charlie, a little proud of his
analysis.

@@b798 p
Charlie smiled and chuckled. "No, Dad. The silver and soybean pits
are squeezed right next to each other. When there is a lot of action in
silver, it spills over to the soybean traders. They see action and want
some of their own. It is just human psychology!"

@@b800 p
Figure: The silver and soybean pits stood side by side, so action in
silver spilled over to the soybean traders.

@@b801 p
And then the line Michael has repeated for fifty years: "You can
figure the dogs, but you can't figure the human race!"

@@b802 p
<strong>What it means.</strong> Before you build a theory about why
two markets are linked, ask who is standing next to whom.

@@b803 p
<strong>Try this.</strong> When two charts move together, write down
one human explanation before you write a mathematical one.

@@b804 h2
11. The man with inside
information

@@b805 p
<em>A contrary-opinion story from 1974 or 1975.</em>

@@b806 p
A man in a very nice suit, carrying a fancy briefcase, walked into
the Peavey office one morning. The manager, Pete Rednor, asked if he
could help. "I have inside information on tomorrow's soybean report. It
is going to be very bullish. I want to purchase ten contracts!"

@@b807 p
Everyone listened. Pete explained that inside information on crop
reports is all but impossible to get, but of course he would take the
business. The man filled out an account card and wrote a check for the
margin. "I am told soybeans will be up the limit at least two days. I
will come back tomorrow before the market opens to hear the report."

@@b808 p
After he left, Michael, Pete, Charlie and two other traders looked at
the soybean chart. Nobody saw anything bullish or bearish. They asked
Charlie what he thought.

@@b809 p
"If this guy who knows nothing about the market has this information,
then it is all over the street and prices are only up a few cents today.
Everyone who wants to buy will buy today. I predict a strong close,
maybe a slightly strong opening, then a sharp sell-off. The report will
be bullish but already in the market."

@@b810 p
He suggested watching the point and figure chart at the opening, on
the minimum tick, for a sell signal. The market closed a little strong.
The man came back early. The report was very bullish, and he smiled at
what he thought was a $2,000-per-contract profit on ten contracts. Then
the market opened up two or three cents, moved sideways, collapsed, and
closed limit down. "Never see man again," Michael wrote.

@@b811 p
<strong>What it means.</strong> News is worth what the market has not
already paid for it. When everyone has the same tip, the tip is already
in the price.

@@b812 p
<strong>Try this.</strong> Before you trade a headline, ask: who
already knows this, and what have they already done?

@@b813 h2
12. The train that did not
jerk

@@b814 p
<em>The Simplot potato scandal, 1976.</em>

@@b815 p
Michael says it helps to search for the Simplot potato scandal of
1976, but that none of the accounts is entirely accurate, and he was
there.

@@b816 p
The May Maine potato contract was nearing delivery. Everyone said
Simplot, a big grower who was heavily short, did not have potatoes to
deliver, so a squeeze was on. Michael traded the "pots" because the
contract was small and its moves were small. He was long three
contracts. Normally May potatoes moved two to four cents a pound. Now
they were moving seven.

@@b817 p
Big longs were also buying up rail cars so Simplot could not ship
potatoes to Chicago. The story ran on the evening news for days. Then
one night the news showed a big train of sixty or seventy cars rolling
into the Chicago yards, all stamped SIMPLOT. Simplot had found potatoes.
Sell, sell. The squeeze was over.

@@b818 p
Charlie called Michael at home right after the news. "Dad, don't sell
your potatoes. Those rail cars are either empty or just partially full.
When they open them, the potato contract goes to 15 or 20 cents. Hold
your position!"

@@b819 p
"How do you know that, Charlie?"

@@b820 p
"Dad, the commodity house in Garden City, Kansas, where I traded as a
young man, was right across from the rail yards. We used to watch the
trains come in as a distraction from slow markets. When boxcars are
heavy with a load, they buck and jerk, because momentum makes them push
and pull. Those Simplot cars are running too steady. I see no jerking or
bucking. They are either empty or just partially full."

@@b822 p
Figure: Loaded boxcars buck and jerk; the Simplot cars ran too
steady, so Charlie judged them empty or partly full.

@@b823 p
Charlie was right. Potatoes soared. Then the New York Mercantile
Exchange declared force majeure and settled the contract at about 8
cents. It killed the potato futures contract. Michael wrote that people
thought, "I can't win even when I am right, why play?" A new contract
was tried two years later and no one would touch it.

@@b824 p
<strong>What it means.</strong> Charlie's edge was a lifetime of
watching small physical details, and the patience to remember them for
forty years.

@@b825 p
<strong>Try this.</strong> Keep a notebook of small things you notice
about how a market moves that are not on your indicators. Review it
monthly.

@@b826 h2
13. Everyone was bearish at
the bottom

@@b827 p
<em>The 1974 low and the market letters.</em>

@@b828 p
The Dow came off a seven-year bear market and bottomed at about 575
in October 1974. Charlie and Michael used to go to Bache and read all
the market letters: Dines, Schultz, Russell, Holt, Myers and others.
They were all doom and gloom. Charlie joked, "You read these, you feel
like going home and blowing your brains out."

@@b829 p
Around 600, Charlie told the Bache brokers to buy, because the market
had bottomed. Only Jerry Peters believed him.

@@b831 p
Figure: The Dow bottomed near 575 in October 1974 while every market
letter was gloomy; Charlie said buy around 600.

@@b832 p
From 1975 to 1980 Michael managed stock accounts for two brokers
while the Dow climbed from 575 to about 800. Charlie had given him a
fundamental ratio, and Goodman plus the ratio served those clients very
well. "Charlie hated technical ratios but seemed OK with some basic
fundamental stuff."

@@b833 p
<strong>What it means.</strong> When every newsletter agrees, you are
looking at a crowd, not at information. Charlie bought where the crowd
could not.

@@b834 p
<strong>Try this.</strong> Count how many analysts in your feed share
your view. If it is nearly all of them, reduce your size.

@@b841 h2
Market Anatomy (2021)

@@b842 p
In this paper I look at the anatomy of the market: who really decides
that price may go higher or lower. My answer is not "buyers" or
"sellers" alone. It is a shared culture that manages price, and it
judges by experiment.

@@b843 h3
Why price rises without
buyers

@@b844 p
Many people believe that when price is very high, it must fall
because buyers are missing. Yet if you watch closely, the market often
moves from one new high to the next even when buyers are few. The reason
is simple: sellers refuse to sell lower than that price.

@@b845 p
I call this the harmonic culture of sellers. They agree together not
to accept a lower, unacceptable price.
