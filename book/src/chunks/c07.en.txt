@@b414 h2
5. You have to find it on your
own

@@b415 p
<em>The night on the mountain pass.</em>

@@b416 p
Michael and Charlie were driving back from the dog track in Colorado
Springs, around midnight, over a mountain pass in Michael's new Datsun
280ZX turbo. Michael was worried the car would stall, as it had a couple
of times. Charlie spoke in the dark.

@@b417 p
"In the late 1950s I met a young fellow who was learning to trade. He
looked like he had promise. I approached him and over several short
meetings I explained the basic 1-2-3, propagation, intersection, and so
on. He seemed a little interested but not particularly connected to it.
I lost track of him."

@@b418 p
Several years later Charlie saw him at a brokerage house and asked
how his trading was going. The man was excited. "I've tried everything
under the sun! But I finally found something that works! The markets are
all 1-2-3 waves and they are interconnected, Charlie! I wait for one to
repeat then hop aboard!"

@@b419 p
Charlie was stunned. It was exactly what he had told the man years
before.

@@b420 p
"Dad, I realized he had to find it on his own. You couldn't tell it
to him, that was a waste of time. Finding something on your own means so
much more than having someone give it to you or explain it to you. This
is why each generation learns anew. Parents tell their kids things and
get ignored, but when the kids find it later on their own, it's
knowledge. That's why I teach you by example rather than a book
explanation like that thing you wrote!"

@@b421 p
Michael later added a note for me: don't explain, use examples and
set an example. When it is time, a smart child will discover it alone.
"Patience, in the markets and in parenting."

@@b422 p
<strong>What it means.</strong> Information can be handed over.
Understanding has to be earned.

@@b423 p
<strong>Try this.</strong> When you explain a setup to someone, show
them two charts and ask what they notice before you say a word.

@@b424 h2
6. "What did you think I
meant?"

@@b425 p
<em>Why the best teacher in the room sounded vague.</em>

@@b426 p
Michael came to Charlie with a Western, rigid, scientific mind.
Charlie's teaching often frustrated him. Charlie would point to a chart
and say, "There's your return, Dad!" Michael would hurry home, go over
the charts, and try to work out where the Returns were. When he showed
his results, Charlie sometimes looked a little puzzled: "What did you
think I meant?"

@@b427 p
There was a reason, which Michael learned later. In the late 1950s
Charlie had tried to teach a student in a Western, step-by-step
delineation style and failed miserably. A few years later he found out
the student had worked out much of it by himself.

@@b428 p
"I just use examples because in the end you have to figure it out for
yourself; you have to find how it fits in your own mind."

@@b429 p
Michael also notes that Charlie had something of an Eastern
mentality, which you can see in the Goodmanisms. His left brain and
right brain communicated extremely well.

@@b430 p
<strong>What it means.</strong> A hint you work out yourself stays in
your head longer than an answer you are given.

@@b431 p
<strong>Try this.</strong> Next time you are stuck, write down what
you think the chart is hinting at, in one sentence, before you ask
anyone.

7. Christmas Day

@@b433 p
<em>The phone call that fixed a mother's anger.</em>

@@b434 p
It happened in Charlie's first or second year with Michael, around
1973 or 1974. On Christmas Day at about one o'clock Michael drove his
parents to Aunt Ruth's family party, a crowded house of twenty-five to
thirty relatives. His mother said she would call at four-thirty or five
to be picked up.

@@b435 p
Michael went home, where it was quiet. The phone rang. It was
Charlie. "Hey, Dad, what are you doing?" He had an idea and wanted to
look at charts. Michael had a couple of hours before his mother would
call, so he drove across town to Charlie's Denver house.

@@b436 p
He lost track of time completely. When he looked at his watch it was
past six. His mother had called and called, and was not happy. She
thought he had gone out raising hell with his friend Mark.

@@b437 p
Charlie told him to go home and then call, and let him talk to his
mother. Michael's mother liked Charlie much more than Mark, who just
looked like trouble to her. Michael did what he was told. His mother was
very angry. He called Charlie. "I don't know what he said to her but
when she got off the phone she was fine and not at all upset with
me!"

@@b438 p
<strong>What it means.</strong> Charlie took responsibility for the
hours he had taken from his student. He solved the problem rather than
letting Michael carry it alone.

@@b439 p
<strong>Try this.</strong> When you ask someone for their time, make
sure they leave better off than they came.

8. Think like Charlie

@@b441 p
<em>An accident outside the window.</em>

@@b442 p
Michael was at Charlie's Denver house one summer afternoon, looking
at charts. Charlie's roll-top desk stood by the window, and the vents
were open. They heard a crash and the crunch of metal. Someone had hit
one of Charlie's Corvairs, probably coming out of the alley across
Fillmore Street. The driver stopped, looked, and drove off before they
got outside.

@@b443 p
The damage was a dented fender and a broken taillight. "What a jerk
for running off!" Michael said.

@@b444 p
Charlie said: "Maybe. But it is possible the accident slowed him down
a few seconds and kept him from being killed by someone running a red
light on Colfax. We really don't know why such things happen and how
they play out eventually."

@@b445 p
"Only Charlie thought like that!!!" Michael wrote.

@@b446 p
<strong>What it means.</strong> A single event looks like an ending
only because we cut the story short. Charlie always asked what else
could be connected to it.

@@b447 p
<strong>Try this.</strong> After your next stop-out, write down one
way the loss may be protecting you from something worse.

@@b448 h2
9. Draw the box before the
setup forms

@@b449 p
<em>Abduction, Charlie's way.</em>

@@b450 p
In July 2025 Michael found an old chart and walked me through one of
Charlie's methods. "After a Setup builds, the most likely consequent for
an Under is the TP of the Jumbo." So Charlie would ask a question while
the Jumbo was still forming: <em>If a Setup formed here, at the current
EP of the Jumbo, how large would it need to be, in vertical value and
with its tail on the Molokai, for an Under count to land at the TP of
the Jumbo?</em> Then he drew a light box on the chart for that Setup
space.

@@b452 p
Figure: While the Jumbo is still forming, a light box marks the space
a Setup would need for an Under count to land at the Jumbo's TP.

@@b453 p
As the Setup began to form, he could then predict how it would build.
Michael is careful: it did not always happen exactly like that, but it
made him extremely proactive with the charts. "I can tell you, many of
them work. Charlie drew a light LayDown and I watched over days and
weeks with my jaw hanging, like he controlled the market." He stresses
the time frame was weeks, not minutes or hours.

@@b454 p
Michael collected eleven of these examples for an article on
Abduction in Goodman Wave Theory. That article and the Playbook, he
said, would be his final word on Goodman. "Everything else is for
you."

@@b455 p
<strong>What it means.</strong> A forecast made before the pattern
forms is a hypothesis you can test. A reaction made after is only a
story.

@@b456 p
<strong>Try this.</strong> On your next Jumbo Swing, draw the box
where a Setup would have to form. Put the date on it. Check it a week
later.

@@b457 h2
The Logic of Goodman (2022)

@@b458 p
In this paper I want to show why traders need logic, and what I call
the logic of Goodman. Logic corrects the mindset. It moves us from
doubt, fear and hope toward clear decisions in a market of probability.
I then show how this logic reads the language of the bar chart, and how
indications on the chart tell the story of buyers and sellers.

@@b459 h3
Introduction

@@b460 p
Traders want to win, because winning serves their goals. They work
for those goals and prepare in every way they can. But many of them lose
the road. They jump from one method to another, or they never fully
understand what their goals really require.

@@b461 p
In the end they doubt their ability to succeed. Some become fearful,
others live on the hope of success. Either way, logic can show them the
real road.

@@b462 h4
Logic corrects the mindset

@@b463 p
Logic guides traders toward success. It is a guide for anyone who
holds wrong premises that block success. It is also a guide for anyone
who carries a wrong belief that came from a bad conclusion or a fallacy
in reasoning.

@@b464 p
Logic corrects our mindset, and through that correction it leads us
to success and to our goals. This is why the logic of Goodman matters so
much.

@@b465 p
A mindset is a perspective: a set of assumptions through which we
look at an environment and make decisions. When the mindset is
corrected, the trader makes the right decisions. He goes long, goes
short, or sits on his hands.

@@b466 h4
Discipline is a lifestyle

@@b467 p
Success comes if, and only if, one has the desire to correct the
mindset based on logic. It is not an easy process. In the end it depends
on your desire, and you must be consistent until you reach discipline in
trading.

@@b468 p
Discipline has its own problem. Once a trader becomes successful, his
intensity drops slowly over the years. Then the door of failure can open
again. So we must refresh and correct the mindset by logic, again and
again. Yes, it is a lifestyle.

@@b469 h4
The aim of the mindset: truth

@@b470 p
What is the aim of a mindset? It is to be the perspective that
explains the truth. It should not only be a valid argument; it should be
true in reality.

@@b471 p
Knowing simply brings what already exists in the world into our
minds. Knowledge makes impressions of things that exist as facts for
us.

@@b472 h4
Two kinds of impression

@@b473 p
An impression can be only an imagination. The chart represents buyers
and sellers, but that picture of buyers and sellers does not lead to a
decision to act. It is an abstract image of a fact that exists.

@@b474 p
An impression can also lead to a decision to act with confidence. For
example, the chart shows a qualified Goldilocks Setup. That impression
is followed by a decision to act.

@@b475 p
Whatever is followed by action, we are responsible for its result. In
the market there are only two actions, buy and sell, and both are under
our control. The market will never force you to enter a trade.

@@b476 p
An impression held with confidence does not necessarily meet the
truth about the market's outcome. Sometimes confidence represents
certainty about the result. Sometimes it represents only the probability
of what is about to happen.

@@b477 h4
Four logical
cases: certainty, doubt and probability

@@b478 p
Let us assume someone expects the Eurxxx pair to move up, and he
invites us to enter the trade. As I said, our actions are our
responsibility.

@@b479 p
Logically, his assumption can only be true, if the move up happens,
or false, if it does not. Our job is to judge whether the assumption is
true or false. So we ask: can this person anticipate the market
correctly 100% of the time? There are four possible answers.

@@b480 blockquote
<p>1. <strong>We decide yes, he can.</strong> Here we have
certainty.</p>
<p>2. <strong>We decide no, he is 100% wrong.</strong> Here we also have
certainty.</p>
<p>3. <strong>We decide he has a 50% chance to be right or
wrong.</strong> Here we have doubt.</p>
<p>4. <strong>We decide he is possibly right that Eurxxx will move
up</strong>, but because of uncertainty we hold delusions about a
possible move down. Here we have only a probability that the outcome
occurs, with some degree of delusion that it will not.</p>

@@b482 p
Figure: Four ways to judge a call: two kinds of certainty, doubt, and
probability with some delusion. Only the fourth meets the reality of the
market.

@@b483 p
So confidence can come with certainty, or with probability, even with
some degree of delusion. Yes, this is the reality of the market.

@@b484 p
Can a man be 100% certain about the outcome of the market? That man
does not exist yet.

@@b485 h4
When a
trader cannot decide: two kinds of ignorance

@@b486 p
What about the trader who still cannot decide and cannot act? He has
ignorance, a lack of knowledge. Simply put, he has no impression to act
on. Here we must define the type of ignorance.

@@b487 ul
<li><p><strong>Simple ignorance.</strong> He has no knowledge of the
market. Or he has knowledge but cannot turn it into a probability of the
outcome. This ignorance leaves no impression to act on. Such a trader
should learn the logic of Goodman.</p></li>
<li><p><strong>Complex ignorance.</strong> He can read the probability
of the market, but he wants certainty about the outcome. He wants to
trade with no delusions. This ignorance does not meet the reality of the
market. It is complex because he feels he knows a great deal, and still
he refuses to act without certainty. Some courage is needed!</p></li>

@@b488 p
By now it should be clear why it is necessary to learn the logic of
Goodman. It gives the trader the ability to decide in an environment of
probability.
