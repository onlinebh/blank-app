@@b153 h3
Probability: What Four
Trades Teach Us

@@b154 p
In most experiments we can list the possible results before they
happen. Each trade has its possible outcomes:

@@b155 p
Group of possibilities = {Win, Loss}; the probability of a Win =
1/2.

@@b156 p
The group of possibilities means considering everything that can
happen before we experiment. Now let us repeat the experiment: we take
four trades.

@@b157 p
Here I lean on James Bernoulli and his book <em>The Art of
Conjecturing</em>:

@@b158 blockquote
<p>"People create their own good or bad fortune by their conduct; for
the most part, those who are wise leave the least possible to chance. We
cannot know the future, but we can always in games of chance, and often
in other affairs of life, know exactly how much more probable it is that
something will happen in one way rather than another. Since these are
the limits of our knowledge, we might at least reach them.</p>
<p>Everyone knows that when certainty is impossible, we should search
for verisimilitude, [some degrees of which] are greater than others to
infinity…"</p>
<p>"A player will enjoy a game more if he knows at each turn what his
hope of winning or risk of losing is."</p>

@@b159 p
To summarize his theory, here are all the ways the wins can fall in
four trades:

@@b160 ul
<li><p><strong>Four wins</strong> — 1 way: {W,W,W,W}</p></li>
<li><p><strong>Three wins</strong> — 4 ways: {W,W,W,L}, {W,W,L,W},
{W,L,W,W}, {L,W,W,W}</p></li>
<li><p><strong>Two wins</strong> — 6 ways: {W,W,L,L}, {W,L,W,L},
{W,L,L,W}, {L,W,W,L}, {L,W,L,W}, {L,L,W,W}</p></li>
<li><p><strong>One win</strong> — 4 ways: {W,L,L,L}, {L,W,L,L},
{L,L,W,L}, {L,L,L,W}</p></li>
<li><p><strong>No win</strong> — 1 way: {L,L,L,L}</p></li>

@@b161 p
In one experiment, a win has a probability of ½ and only one way to
appear. When we repeat the experiment four times, the wins have many
different ways to appear.

@@b162 p
Four wins in a row has only one way to appear, {W,W,W,W}. It is the
least probable result in statistics, and yet it is what traders hope
for. Four losses in a row, {L,L,L,L}, also has only one way. It is just
as improbable, and yet it is what most traders fear.

@@b163 p
One win out of four and three wins out of four both have four ways to
appear, so they are equal in probability. Only one result has the
highest probability, with six ways to appear: two wins in four
trades.

@@b164 p
The benchmark for Goodman traders is 10-4-1. Statistics almost
confirm it as the high-probability approach, as the four-trade
experiment shows. So set the risk for ten trades, let the winners come
by themselves, and learn to trade the Line to increase the
probability.

@@b165 h3
Why a Trader Needs a Method

@@b166 p
This short introduction is the base before I explain Goodman's
theory.

@@b167 p
The theory is a process. It interprets the market's fluctuations as
phenomena, connects them with their original cause, and explains the
factors that govern them.

@@b168 p
For the follower of this theory, it becomes the method he prefers to
apply in the market. The method solves the problems traders usually face
when they start to apply the theory. On this basis, I cannot imagine a
community of traders without a method to organize their activity in the
market.

@@b169 p
Without any doubt, traders always want to choose the right method,
one that makes them more profitable and is easy to apply. But statistics
show that most traders lose and very few profit consistently. Do the
losers use a losing method? Or do they pick anything and call it a
method, without deep study?

@@b170 p
The right method always has practical proof, or at least a true logic
behind it. That logic encourages the trader to use it, profit from it,
and trade with confidence because he accepts and believes it. A method
without confidence leaves room for doubt, and doubt always stops action.
No action, no success.

@@b171 h3
1.1
How Human Behavior Changed Through the History of Trading

@@b172 p
Trading today plays a primary role in the economy. Human needs grow
as new and creative items come to market. Suppliers specialize in one
item and neglect the others, so that they can compete.

@@b173 p
From this, people get what they need and the economy expands. Then
the economic cycle sets in: prosperity, crisis, depression. Crisis is
followed by depression, depression by prosperity, and prosperity by
crisis again.

@@b174 p
Let me try to explain these cycles briefly. Any exchange happens in
one of two ways:

@@b175 ul
<li><p><strong>Barter</strong>: exchanging goods directly.</p></li>
<li><p><strong>A medium of exchange</strong>, such as money.</p></li>

@@b176 h4
Barter

@@b177 p
Barter is the oldest way: you simply exchange 10 kg of cotton for 10
kg of wheat. But the system cannot continue when someone wants fruit and
offers cotton, while the fruit seller does not want cotton.

@@b178 p
In barter, buyer and seller exchange their benefits at the same time.
Both are satisfied in one transaction. Supply and demand stay roughly in
balance. People satisfy their own needs first, then exchange the
remaining cotton and fruit for other things they need, without inflation
in price.

@@b179 h4
Money

@@b180 p
Human needs were the basis for exchange with money. With money, the
difficulty of exchanging fruit for cotton is no longer a problem. But
money brought its own difficulties, and as we moved from barter to
money, many things in economic history changed with it.

@@b181 p
With money, both sides must agree on a market price. Each must first
sell to gain money before he can buy what he needs. So each plays the
role of seller to gain money, and then the role of buyer. That creates
the desire to collect money.

@@b182 p
Buyers and sellers then found new ways to collect money. Instead of a
direct exchange, a buyer takes the item and pays later on credit.
Sellers also pay their suppliers on credit. Everyone seeks to collect
money instead of having a real desire for the items.

@@b183 p
Money can also be saved and kept, while fruit is hard to keep for
long. And money can be borrowed and returned with interest.

@@b184 p
So the relationship between production and consumption changed into
one between production and collecting money. The seller produces and
exchanges for money in order to collect more money. The buyer pays money
for items as an expense, not to re-produce or to satisfy needs.

@@b185 p
This brought an imbalance between the quantity demanded and the
quantity supplied. Human behavior changed once money appeared. Instead
of exchanging for needs, one can now buy for a monopoly, to raise or to
push down the price, without any real desire for the item. The small
capital of small buyers and sellers is always the fish eaten by the
larger fish, whose purpose is to push small competitors out of the
market.

@@b186 blockquote
<p>"Yellow coins and white money are able to destroy the will
power."</p>

@@b187 p
In brief, this section shows how people in general behave and act:
they simply seek the maximum profit they can catch. This forms the basis
for anticipating the upcoming move.

@@b188 h3
1.2 The Repetition of Human
Behavior

@@b189 p
Almost every time people find an opportunity to gain more money, they
take it together, in consensus. They continue until fear sets in with
the idea that this is the maximum profit the opportunity can give. Then
a decline follows.

@@b190 p
On the contrary, when they cannot gain from the trade, they withdraw
gradually. Profit acts as a magnet and loss acts as a repellent. This
behavior repeats over and over.

@@b191 p
Goodman's theory interprets the market. The applied theory
anticipates what will most likely happen, based on that interpretation,
and it provides a technique to manage the trade.

@@b192 h4
Interpreting One Bar

@@b193 p
Suppose we have a daily bar. Our first job is to interpret it.

@@b195 p
Figure: One bar read as a contest — the distance from Low to Close is
the buyers' gain, the distance from High to Close is the sellers' gain,
and volume expresses the will of buying.

@@b196 p
Price rose because of an imbalance between demand and supply. In this
bar the buyers were looking for profit. At the close, measured from the
low of the bar, the buyers gained. Measured from the high to the close,
the sellers lost.

@@b197 p
The distance from Low to Close (the buying side) is greater than the
distance from High to Close (the selling side). So the buyers gained
more in this bar. Volume shows the quantity of transactions the buyers
paid for. It expresses the will of buying.

@@b198 p
Do not anticipate from one bar. Always look for a combination: an
Overlay. When buyers repeat their behavior and keep the will to buy for
more profit, from the first bar to many swings and Matrices, Propagation
starts. When you interpret bars, always remember that buyers and sellers
change their will power.

@@b199 h4
Bars 1 to 6: Buyers in
Control

@@b201 p
Figure: Bars 1 to 6 between $10 and $15 — buyers repeat their
purchases, bars 1-3 close at the high, and bar 4 is the first test of
their will on low volume.

@@b202 p
Buyers repeat their purchases with growing will in bars 1, 2 and 3.
All of these bars close at the high, with gains. The picture the bars
give is that buying is still strong and looking for more.

@@b203 p
In bar 4, sellers interrupt the buyers' gains for a moment. This is
the first examination of the buyers' will: will they continue and hold
their gains? The low volume in this bar says the sellers have very
little stock to offer, and buyers would have to pay for it. In bars 1, 2
and 3 the buyers were able to pay for similar quantities.

@@b204 p
The deduction from these facts: the probability is greater on the
buyers' side. Buyers are still happy with their gains. But nothing
continues forever. In bars 5 and 6 buyers hold the gain and look for
more profit, up to $15.

@@b205 p
Every change in the market either creates a new plan or tells us to
watch carefully, because the plan may change soon. When we add a few new
bars to the last deduction, the plan does change.

@@b209 p
In bar 7 sellers repeat their effort to stop the buyers' gains, with

Bar 8 has the smallest range from high to low. Buyers are gradually

@@b215 blockquote
<p>"A period of prosperity inevitably comes to an end because cost
ultimately creeps up on selling prices so far as to reduce the margin of
profits and hence to discourage further investments."</p>

@@b216 p
Profit is always the magnet, the axis of their activity. Fear drives
the buyers in bar 7, and in bar 8 they withdraw from the market.

@@b217 p
Bar 12 is the most ambiguous case. It is an Intersection with the 50%
level. Buyers who entered at bar 1 and held now see 50% of their gain
lost. Sellers have 50% of the gain in progress. It is a competition over
who can gain more.

@@b218 p
In bar 12 the volume increased while the range decreased: buyers
stepped in to protect their gains. Compare it with the volume in bars 10
and 11. If buyers have stepped in, we wait for a response in the next
bar, the response that removes our doubt.

@@b219 blockquote
<p>"But depression, like prosperity, has its own limits. Demand cannot
shrink indefinitely, for consumers must be fed, clothed, and housed. All
the elements in cost decline—raw materials, wages, interest, expenses of
getting established. Further, the depression stimulates efforts to
improve methods of production."</p>

@@b223 p
In bar 13 the buyers reinforce their will. Now they can buy any stock

behavior is the key.

@@b224 h4
From Bars to Swings

@@b225 p
From the low of bar 1 to the high of bar 7, the last high price, is
the first swing. From bar 7 to bar 13 is the secondary swing, down to
the last low the market reached.

@@b227 p
Figure: The same bars grouped into FPS (BP to EP 1) and SS (EP 1 to
EP 2), with the volume box and the time of each swing compared.

@@b228 p
Now we can observe that all bars within the FPS represent the
consensus of buyers and their abilities. All bars within the SS
represent the consensus of sellers and their abilities. At the 50% level
there is competition, and the buyers overcome the weaker desire of the
sellers, as bar 13 shows.

@@b229 p
From BP to EP of the FPS there is the greater gain and the greater
ability, seen in the total volume box of the FPS. The SS has the lowest
gain and less ability: sellers offer stock that buyers will not pay for.
Compare also the time buyers spent enhancing their profit.

@@b230 p
The deduction from these facts: the most probable outcome is that the
next several bars will make the SPS. With Goodman's theory one can
anticipate several bars at once. With the 3-C one can see where buyers
or sellers are most likely to lose their will. The Intersection works
like a police checkpoint: only the side with the greatest ability to
propagate moves forward. It is a very competitive point, as in bars 12
and 13.

@@b232 p
Figure: FPS, SS and the anticipated SPS together form the 1-2-3, the
Matrix of GWT.

@@b233 p
The 1-2-3 is called a Matrix in GWT. It is composed of three swings:
FPS, SS and SPS.

@@b234 p
In summary:

@@b235 blockquote
<p>1. A few bars make a swing.</p>
<p>2. A few swings make a 1-2-3.</p>
<p>3. Each 1-2-3 is a Matrix.</p>
<p>4. A combination of Matrices is the lens of Goodman's theory.</p>
